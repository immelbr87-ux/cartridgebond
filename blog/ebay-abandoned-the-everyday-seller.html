eBay's Trust Problem. And What Comes Next.

May 18, 20265 min read

A piece circulating this week surfaced a number worth sitting with: eBay collected $2 billion last year from sellers paying to promote their own listings on a platform those sellers already pay fees to use.

The piece frames this as a fee problem. We think it is a trust problem. And the distinction matters.

$2BeBay ad revenue from its own sellers in 2025
0%Growth in "enthusiast buyers" over 4 straight years
~13%Fees a Switch seller pays before visibility costs

The problem is not fees. The problem is alignment.

Marketplaces charging fees is not the issue. Fees fund the infrastructure that makes trust possible. Verified identities, dispute resolution, secure payments - none of that is free, and sellers who want it should expect to pay for it.

The issue with eBay's model is different. When a platform charges sellers a separate fee just to be seen by the buyers already on the platform, the incentives are inverted. The platform profits whether or not a transaction happens. The seller pays twice and takes all the risk.

"Sellers paid $2 billion just to make sure their own listings showed up. The platform earned whether the item sold or not."

Aligned fee models look different. A buyer trust fee, charged at transaction, means the platform earns only when a match succeeds. A promoted listing option, offered transparently and priced fairly, gives sellers a tool - not a tax on visibility.

CartridgeBond is in beta with zero fees while we prove the model. As the platform grows, the fee model grows with it. We plan to introduce a small buyer fee at transaction - one that funds real trust infrastructure rather than platform profit.

Post-beta, that infrastructure includes escrow. Both buyer and seller are protected: funds are held until the exchange is complete and condition is confirmed. If a buyer flakes, they are charged a cancellation fee that compensates the seller for their time. If a game arrives misrepresented - wrong condition, missing items - the dispute triggers the same protection. The platform absorbs the enforcement cost and pays the affected party. Sellers will always net the full locked price.

That is what aligned fees look like. The platform earns when trust is delivered, not when a listing appears.

The bigger picture: precommerce

The eBay story points to something larger than one company's fee structure. Traditional marketplaces run on post-commerce logic: list your item, hope the right buyer finds it, negotiate a price, then try to complete the transaction. Every step is a new opportunity for friction, no-shows, and price erosion.

Precommerce inverts the sequence. Both parties agree on terms before anyone moves. The price is locked. The condition is verified. The meetup is confirmed. By the time two parties connect to complete the exchange, the transaction is already done in every way that matters.

We built CartridgeBond for Nintendo Switch games in Milwaukee because games are a low-friction way to prove the model. Small items. Established resale values. A community that already trades them. But the mechanics scale to anything where trust and price volatility are the primary friction: electronics, collectibles, sporting goods, instruments.

The reason eBay's buyer base has not grown in four years is not a marketing problem. It is a trust problem. Buyers do not trust that the item will match the listing. Sellers do not trust that the buyer will show up. Platforms that solve for trust before the transaction - not after - are where commerce goes next.

What this week means

eBay's board rejected a $56 billion takeover bid from Ryan Cohen, the GameStop chair, who publicly called out the company's strategy of harvesting its seller base instead of growing it. Whether or not the bid succeeds, the conversation is now public: the traditional marketplace model has a trust ceiling, and the market knows it.

CartridgeBond is a small beta in one city. We are not eBay. But we are building toward a model where the platform earns only when trust is earned, and where the seller walks away with the price they were promised.
